Key resources and activities: assets are not actions

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

MechanismWorked exampleUnderstand and applySelf-checkSources

Canvas operating design

Mechanism and distinctions

A key resource is an important asset needed for a business model to work; a key activity is something the organization must do. A software license is an intellectual resource; developing or maintaining software is an activity. A skilled team is a human resource; solving a particular customer problem is an activity. These are complementary descriptions, not competing labels for the same list.

Work backwards from the value proposition, channels, relationships and revenue logic: what must be available, and what must be done reliably? Physical, intellectual, human and financial resources may all matter. “Key” means important for this design, not simply expensive or owned. Leased infrastructure and resources accessed through partners can be essential even when the firm does not own them. Access conditions and dependence therefore belong in the model.

The activity categories production, problem solving and platform/network highlight different operating logics. Production concerns designing, making and delivering products; problem solving develops responses to individual customer problems and may require knowledge management and training; platform work includes management, service provisioning and promotion of a shared platform. A digital firm can combine categories. Merely buying servers does not perform matching, resolve disputes or maintain a service.

Resource quantity is not yet effective capacity. A resource-to-activity model must state available productive time, processing requirements, demand and the promised service level. The simple bottleneck calculations below are own teaching applications, not a capacity formula supplied by the Canvas. They assume divisible work, compatible skills, no other bottleneck and constant processing times; real waiting times, peaks and quality can invalidate those simplifications.

Worked example

Own hypothetical service case, not an official exercise. A design-support platform promises 240 reviewed requests per month. It has four specialists, each with 60 productive hours per month, and 0.75 specialist-hour is needed per request. Specialist time is a human resource; reviewing requests is a problem-solving activity. Software and licensed knowledge are intellectual resources; running access and matching are platform activities.

Available time = 4 × 60 = 240 specialist-hours/month. Review capacity = 240/0.75 = 320 requests/month. At demand 240, required time = 180 hours/month and unused time = 60 hours/month. This is 75% utilization of the stated productive time, not proof that every customer receives an immediate response. Time already excluded from the 60-hour allowance must not be subtracted again.

If a more complex offer takes 1.25 hours/request, the same resources provide only 192 requests/month. The 240-request promise then exceeds capacity by 48 requests, or 60 hours/month. At least five identical specialists are needed in this simplified model: 240 × 1.25/60 = 5. Acquiring a resource and changing an activity are different interventions; neither establishes demand or service quality by itself.

Understanding pathway

Own exercises, not official assignments. Write your attempt before opening a hint or solution. Assess reasoning, units and interpretation against the criteria; a number without a derivation is not a complete answer. Open responses are self-assessed, never validated by keyword matching.

1. Reconstruct a resource–activity chain

Your attempt: Without looking back, map a remote diagnostic service from its customer promise to one human, one intellectual and one physical resource, then two activities. Explain why ownership is not the deciding criterion.

Optional hint

Use nouns for assets and verbs for actions; test each against delivery of the promise.

Complete worked answer and criteria

For a timely remote diagnostic report: trained analysts are human resources, validated diagnostic knowledge/software licenses are intellectual resources, and equipment is physical. Analysing evidence and maintaining the secure service are activities. Leased equipment and partner-provided licenses can still be key because reliable access, not ownership alone, enables delivery. Other coherent mappings are acceptable.

Assessment: Resource types and plausible mapping (2), two distinct actions (1), access versus ownership (1): 4 points.

2. Calculate a changed processing requirement

Your attempt: Own case: three specialists each provide 72 productive hours/month. Each request requires 0.9 hour, and demand is 260 requests/month. Compute capacity, required hours, shortage and the minimum whole specialist count. Interpret the result.

Optional hint

Keep request units separate from hours, then round the staffing requirement upwards.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Available time = 216 hours/month. Capacity = 216/0.9 = 240 requests/month. Demand requires 234 hours/month, so the shortage is 18 hours or 20 requests/month. Required headcount = ceiling(234/72) = 4 specialists. Four provide 288 hours, hence a discrete surplus of 54 hours at that demand. This is a deterministic workload check, not a waiting-time or quality guarantee.

Assessment: 240 capacity; 234 hours; 18-hour/20-request shortage; four specialists with upward rounding; units and model boundary: 1 point each, 5 points.

3. Diagnose the server purchase

Your attempt: A proposal says: “We bought twice as many servers, so customer problem-solving capacity has doubled. Our licensed software is a key activity.” Identify two independent errors and the missing evidence.

Optional hint

Look for the actual bottleneck and an asset/action confusion.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Server capacity does not automatically double specialist capacity; time, skill and workflow may remain the bottleneck. Measure productive specialist time, processing requirements and any actual server constraint. A license is access to an intellectual resource, not an activity; using, updating and maintaining software are actions. The relevant promise determines which is key.

Assessment: Bottleneck error (1), relevant evidence (1), license/resource correction (1), action counterpart linked to promise (1): 4 points.

4. Transfer to a mixed workload

Your attempt: Return to four specialists × 60 hours/month. Of 240 requests, 60% need 0.5 hour and 40% need 1.5 hours. Calculate total time and utilization. Then suppose the complex share becomes 70% at unchanged demand. Is the promise feasible? Propose a targeted response and a boundary.

Optional hint

Weight hours per request by the mix; do not average the two times equally.

Complete worked answer and criteria

First mean = 0.6 × 0.5 + 0.4 × 1.5 = 0.9 hour/request. Total = 216 hours/month; utilization = 90%. With 70% complex, mean = 0.3 × 0.5 + 0.7 × 1.5 = 1.2 hours, so total = 288 hours and utilization of existing time = 120%; shortage = 48 hours/month. A fifth identical specialist provides 300 hours, making the workload feasible in this model. Reducing complex-case time through a validated workflow is another conditional option; quality and peak demand must still be tested.

Assessment: Weighted 0.9 hour; 216 hours/90%; new 288 hours/120%; 48-hour shortfall and targeted intervention; quality/peak boundary: 1 point each, 5 points.

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record whether your first error concerned classification, units, workload mix or a service guarantee. Tomorrow, rebuild the resource–activity chain and solve the three-specialist case without notes. In one week, change the complex-case share and identify the new bottleneck before calculating.
