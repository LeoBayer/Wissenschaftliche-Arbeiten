Visibility: awareness and public shaming

IT-driven Innovation · Course 96437 · WiSe 2026/27

Explain the mechanism

Visibility changes who can observe an action. Livestreaming can make an event visible to participants and a public audience, but visibility is neither verification nor a verdict. An affordance analysis must name the artifact, the possible action and the actors rather than attach “good” or “bad” permanently to a feature.

The lecture’s fourth case concerns volunteer groups responding to perceived institutional failure in addressing online child sexual exploitation. It describes civic engagement alongside vigilantism, public shaming and collateral harm. This learning page analyses that ambivalence; it is not a guide to conducting confrontations. An arrest mentioned in a case account is not equivalent to a finding of guilt, and public exposure can affect families and other people beyond an accused individual.

The focused lecture graphic keeps the technical feature, action possibility, actors and two contrasting consequences together. Scroll horizontally on narrow screens; the region can also be focused and scrolled with arrow keys.

Livestreaming affords visibility to the groups and audience. Transparency and community awareness are possible benefits; public shaming and reputational violence are possible harms. Neither follows from the camera alone: actor goals, moral framings and the surrounding sociotechnical setting shape actual use. “More visible” therefore does not mean “more justified.” The graphic is a conceptual comparison, not a measured benefit–harm balance.

For governance analysis, separate preserving information for appropriate review from exposing identifiable people to an unlimited audience. A restricted review channel is a hypothetical design alternative, not a guarantee of correctness or a legal prescription. Removing public broadcasting changes visibility; it does not automatically establish fair review, eliminate leaks or resolve every coordination problem.

Worked example

Own fictional platform-review case, not evidence from the study. A platform receives 40 incident reports. Ten reports are duplicates of ten other reports; assume every other report concerns a separate incident. There are 30 distinct incidents. Of these, 18 have enough supporting material to be sent for further review; 12 do not yet meet that threshold. The reviewed share is 18/30 = 60% of distinct incidents, but 18/40 = 45% of all submitted reports. Neither is a guilt rate.

Suppose the platform publicly displays all 30 incidents before review. Twelve distinct incidents are exposed without even meeting its initial evidence threshold. Restricting public display to zero while retaining all 40 reports in an access-controlled review channel reduces this specific exposure count to zero under the stated no-leak assumption. It does not make the 18 reports true or show that harm has been eliminated. The worked distinction is between information retained, review status and public visibility, not between punishment levels.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment or examination questions. Write your attempt before opening a hint. Use the complete solution and concrete criteria to assess your reasoning; no keyword matching is used. A correct number without its requested reasoning does not earn reasoning credit.

1. Rebuild the actor-specific analysis

Your attempt: Without looking back, identify the feature, affordance, actors, one bright outcome and one dark outcome. Explain why visibility alone cannot choose between them.

Optional hint

Distinguish an action possibility from its consequences.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Livestreaming is the feature; visibility is the affordance; groups and public audiences are relevant actors. Awareness/transparency and shaming/reputational harm are contrasting outcomes. Goals, framing and social arrangements influence which consequences arise. Visibility itself does not verify claims.

Assessment: Feature and affordance (1), actors (1), contrasting outcomes (1), conditional mechanism (1): 4 points.

2. Recalculate a review funnel

Your attempt: Own case: 50 submitted reports contain 10 duplicate reports. Twenty-four of the 40 distinct incidents pass an initial review threshold. Calculate the pass share using each denominator and the number of distinct incidents below threshold. Explain what none of these numbers proves.

Optional hint

Remove duplicate reports once, then keep denominators explicit.

Complete worked answer and criteria

There are 50 − 10 = 40 distinct incidents. The shares are 24/40 = 60% of distinct incidents and 24/50 = 48% of all reports. Sixteen distinct incidents are below threshold. Passing an initial review threshold is not proof of truth or guilt, and failing it is not proof an incident never occurred.

Assessment: 40 incidents, 60%, 48%, 16 incidents, evidential interpretation: 1 point each (5).

3. Diagnose visibility as evidence

Your attempt: A dashboard states: “Our streams attracted many viewers, and several cases led to arrests; therefore every exposed person was guilty and all exposure was beneficial.” Find two independent inferential gaps.

Optional hint

Audience size and procedural events answer different questions from truth and net impact.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Viewership measures attention, not evidential validity or a fair assessment of an individual. An arrest is not a finding of guilt, and selected outcomes cannot establish that every exposure was correct or beneficial. Evaluate review quality and harms to affected people separately; the case summary alone supplies no universal success rate.

Assessment: Attention versus validity (1), arrest versus guilt (1), non-universal inference (1), separate harm/review assessment (1): 4 points.

4. Transfer to restricted reporting

Your attempt: A redesigned platform retains private reporting and group coordination but removes public livestreaming. What mechanism is reduced, what remains, and how would you assess a trade-off?

Optional hint

Change only the feature actually removed.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Public real-time visibility and its associated exposure pathway are reduced. Private coordination and information collection remain; inaccurate reports, leaks and group reinforcement can remain too. Compare appropriate review completion and delays alongside unwanted disclosure and affected-person harm. Reduced public awareness may be a trade-off, not proof the redesign is worse. A causal claim needs a credible comparison and attention to other changes.

Assessment: Changed visibility (1), retained coordination (1), remaining risks (1), balanced evaluation (1), causal limit (1): 5 points.

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record whether you confused attention, review and truth. Tomorrow, reconstruct the feature–affordance–actor chain and redo the 50-report case. In one week, analyse delayed publication instead of private-only reporting. Distinguish a corrected answer from the reason your first attempt failed.
