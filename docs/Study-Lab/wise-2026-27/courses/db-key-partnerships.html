Key partnerships: motives, forms and remaining dependence

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

MechanismWorked exampleUnderstand and applySelf-checkSources

Canvas operating design

Mechanism and distinctions

A key partnership describes a relationship through which a business model obtains resources or activities it needs. Start with the dependency: what must be supplied, coordinated or made accessible for the customer promise to work? A long supplier list alone does not explain which relationships are key.

Keep form and motive separate. Forms include strategic alliances between non-competitors, cooperation between competitors (coopetition), joint ventures for new businesses, and buyer–supplier relationships for reliable supplies. Motives include optimization/economies of scale, reducing risk and uncertainty, and acquiring particular resources or activities such as knowledge, licenses or customer access. One relationship can serve several motives; its form does not guarantee its outcome.

Sharing infrastructure can spread costs, but coordination itself uses resources. Buying an activity can reduce internal workload without eliminating the activity from the overall value-creation system. Licensing software provides access rather than complete control over future terms. Partnering can reduce one risk while creating another dependency: service interruption, renewal terms or a difficult switch may still threaten the promise. These are analytic extensions to test, not claims that every partnership has failed.

A comparison must use the same volume, time horizon and service requirements. A lower per-transaction price is not necessarily a lower total cost when setup, coordination and transition costs differ. Expected-loss calculations below are own simplified decision tools: stated probabilities and losses are assumptions, not observed evidence. Equal expected cost would not imply equal tail risk or equal quality, and a monetary comparison cannot replace checking operational compatibility.

Worked example

Own hypothetical one-month comparison, not an official exercise. A platform needs 2,000 identity checks/month. Internal delivery costs €4,000/month of avoidable capacity plus €2/check. A partner charges €500/month plus €3/check; coordination costs another €600/month. Assume identical quality, compatible timing, no setup costs and genuinely avoidable internal capacity costs over this horizon.

Internal cost = 4,000 + 2 × 2,000 = €8,000/month. Partner cost = 500 + 600 + 3 × 2,000 = €7,100/month. Conditional saving = €900/month. The partner supplies an activity and possibly specialist knowledge; the organization still owns the obligation to deliver its customer promise.

With volume q checks/month, internal cost is 4,000 + 2q and partner cost is 1,100 + 3q euros/month. Equality gives q = 2,900 checks/month; below that volume the partner is cheaper under these assumptions, above it internal delivery is cheaper. If the €4,000 cannot actually be avoided, it cannot be counted as a cash saving from outsourcing. The threshold is a scenario comparison, not a recommendation to sign a contract.

Understanding pathway

Own exercises, not official assignments. Write your attempt before opening a hint or solution. Assess reasoning, units and interpretation against the criteria; a number without a derivation is not a complete answer. Open responses are self-assessed, never validated by keyword matching.

1. Separate motive from partnership form

Your attempt: Explain the difference between a partnership’s form and its motive. Give a plausible coopetition case with two motives and one activity that remains the firm’s responsibility.

Optional hint

Competitors can cooperate in one layer while competing in another.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Two competing service platforms could share a technical compatibility infrastructure while competing for customers. Coopetition names the form; sharing cost and acquiring compatible access are distinct motives. Each platform must still manage its own customer promise and incident response. The example is a hypothetical design, not a claim about real competitors.

Assessment: Form/motive distinction (1), coherent competitor cooperation (1), two motives (1), remaining responsibility (1): 4 points.

2. Recompute the volume crossover

Your attempt: Use the worked example’s rates. At 4,000 checks/month, compute both costs and their difference. Derive the equality volume and state the cheaper side of the threshold.

Optional hint

Subtract cost functions before interpreting the sign.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Internal = €4,000 + €8,000 = €12,000/month. Partner = €1,100 + €12,000 = €13,100/month, so partner delivery costs €1,100 more. Equality: 4,000 + 2q = 1,100 + 3q gives q = 2,900 checks/month. Partner is cheaper below 2,900 and internal above, assuming comparable service and avoidable internal costs.

Assessment: €12,000; €13,100; €1,100 difference with direction; 2,900 and threshold direction; assumptions/units: 1 point each, 5 points.

3. Diagnose risk elimination and sunk savings

Your attempt: A team says: “A partner removes all delivery risk, and our already-paid annual internal lease is a saving next month.” Diagnose both claims and specify evidence needed before comparing options.

Optional hint

Risk transfer is not the same as risk disappearance; ask what cash flow changes in the decision horizon.

Complete worked answer and criteria

A partner may reduce internal operational risk while creating dependency on the partner and coordination; evaluate service reliability, fallback and switching feasibility. An already-paid nonrefundable lease does not disappear next month, so it is not an avoidable next-month saving. Check cancellation/subletting rights and the actual horizon, distinguishing expenditure already incurred from changeable costs.

Assessment: Residual risk (1), relevant reliability/fallback evidence (1), nonavoidable lease correction (1), contractual/horizon evidence (1): 4 points.

4. Transfer to interruption exposure

Your attempt: Own case: A costs €7,100/month, with a 10% monthly chance of an interruption costing €8,000. B costs €7,600/month, with a 2% chance of the same loss. Compute expected monthly cost for each. Then B requires an extra one-time €1,200 transition cost. Compare a one-month horizon and a 12-month horizon with unchanged monthly figures. State a limitation.

Optional hint

Add probability × loss; count the transition cost once, not every month.

Complete worked answer and criteria

A: 7,100 + 0.10 × 8,000 = €7,900/month. B: 7,600 + 0.02 × 8,000 = €7,760/month before transition. One month including transition: B = €8,960 versus A = €7,900, so A is €1,060 lower. Over 12 months: A = €94,800; B = 12 × 7,760 + 1,200 = €94,320, hence B is €480 lower in expectation. Assumptions hold probabilities, volumes and loss sizes constant; expected cost is not a guarantee or a judgment of intolerable service disruption. Independence across months is not needed to add expectations, but correlated disruption can change total-risk exposure.

Assessment: €7,900 and €7,760 (1), correct one-time cost/€8,960 (1), €1,060 one-month difference (1), 12-month totals/€480 (1), uncertainty and service limit (1): 5 points.

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record whether you mixed a form with a motive, counted a nonavoidable cost as a saving, or treated expected loss as certainty. Tomorrow, derive the 2,900-check crossover unaided. In one week, change the transition cost and explain why the preferred horizon may change.
