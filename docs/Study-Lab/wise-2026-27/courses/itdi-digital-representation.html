Digital representation: data must be usable

IT-driven Innovation · Course 96437 · WiSe 2026/27

MechanismWorked exampleUnderstand and applySelf-checkSources

Explain the mechanism

Digital representation turns information, processes or real-world objects into digital form. Its value depends on what operations that representation supports. A collection can contain the right evidence and still be organized in a way that makes the relevant search impractical. Storing a scanned form is not the same as representing its fields in a searchable structure.

The lecture uses the 1911 Mona Lisa theft to illustrate this difference: a fingerprint was available, but the records were organized by body measurements rather than searchable fingerprint characteristics. The lesson is about representation and retrieval, not a claim that every modern query identifies the right person. A database needs comparable features and a suitable search procedure; ambiguous or poor-quality observations can still produce uncertain matches.

Four possibilities reinforce one another. Searchability and organization support retrieval. Standardization makes separately produced records easier to integrate. Scalability and replicability allow additional records and copies; replication can support redundancy but also replicate an error. Automation lets a procedure run repeatedly with less manual intervention. None establishes that the underlying data is complete, accurate or fit for a different purpose.

For example, a date stored as free text may be readable by a person but not consistently sortable. A standard field makes automated comparisons possible only when its meaning is shared. A common field name is not enough if one system records delivery date and another invoice date. Representation therefore links technical format to organizational interpretation. Its affordances are conditional on the actor’s task, data quality and surrounding practices.

A useful analysis follows this chain: real-world event → chosen attributes → digital record → supported operation → interpreted result. Ask where information is lost and which assumptions travel with a record. A search result is an input to judgement, not a guarantee that the intended real-world object has been correctly identified.

Worked example

Own fictional records case, not historical performance data. An archive has 1,200 scanned delivery forms. Staff transcribe the delivery-date field from 900 forms; the other 300 have no structured date. Among the transcribed records, 45 dates are invalid and 855 valid. Structured coverage is 900/1,200 = 75%, but valid structured coverage is 855/1,200 = 71.25%. Within the transcribed subset, validity is 855/900 = 95%.

An automatic date filter can use the 855 valid structured records under the stated rule. It cannot conclude that the other 345 forms contain no relevant deliveries: 300 are unstructured and 45 require correction. Keep those records in an explicit unresolved group. The three percentages answer different questions; “95% valid” is not “95% of the archive searchable.”

Even a syntactically valid date may have been copied from the wrong field. A manual sample should compare the structured date with the intended delivery-date meaning. Copies and faster queries can magnify both useful retrieval and systematic mistakes.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment or examination questions. Write your attempt before opening a hint. Use the complete solution and concrete criteria to assess your reasoning; no keyword matching is used. A correct number without its requested reasoning does not earn reasoning credit.

1. Rebuild the representation chain

Your attempt: Without the page, explain why a scanned form and a structured delivery-date record afford different operations. Connect all four possibilities to a condition that can limit them.

Optional hint

Separate storing pixels, representing a field and interpreting its meaning.

Complete worked answer and criteria

A scan stores an image; a structured date permits filtering/sorting if the extraction is usable. Search needs relevant attributes; integration needs shared formats and meaning; replication needs controlled versions and corrections; automation needs a reliable rule and adequate inputs. A query result still needs interpretation against the task.

Assessment: Scan/field distinction (1), search/standardization conditions (1), replication/automation conditions (1), interpretation limit (1): 4 points.

2. Audit coverage with explicit denominators

Your attempt: Own case: 2,000 forms, 1,600 transcribed dates, 80 invalid transcribed dates. Compute structured coverage, valid structured coverage, validity within transcribed records and unresolved count. Explain why unresolved does not mean irrelevant.

Optional hint

Subtract invalid records only from the transcribed pool; keep the whole-archive denominator for coverage.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Valid structured records: 1,600 − 80 = 1,520. Structured coverage: 80%; valid structured coverage: 76%; validity within transcribed records: 95%. Unresolved: 400 unstructured + 80 invalid = 480 records. Their relevance is unknown, not disproved by failure to enter the filter.

Assessment: 80%, 76%, 95%, 480, relevance/unknown interpretation: 1 point each (5).

3. Find a semantic and a coverage error

Your attempt: An analyst says: “Both systems have a date column, so merging is safe; 95% validity means the whole archive is 95% searchable.” Diagnose two independent problems and suggest checks.

Optional hint

A matching label is not shared meaning; validity has a subset denominator.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Check whether both columns represent the same event, unit and convention, not just the same name. Separately calculate valid records relative to the whole archive rather than only transcribed records. Sample records against originals and retain an unresolved category instead of silently dropping it.

Assessment: Semantic problem (1), meaning check (1), denominator problem (1), whole-archive/unresolved check (1): 4 points.

4. Transfer from dates to quantities

Your attempt: Dates are now complete, but one supplier records quantity in boxes and another in individual items. Design an integration rule and identify a failure it cannot solve automatically.

Optional hint

Shared syntax can conceal different units; box size may itself vary.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Keep original value and unit, obtain a documented items-per-box conversion for each relevant product/version, and convert only when that factor is known. Mark unknown or conflicting factors for review. Test a sample against source records and compare totals with units. This addresses unit compatibility, not incorrect source counts or missing deliveries; do not invent a universal box size.

Assessment: Preserved units (1), product-specific conversion (1), unresolved handling (1), sample/total check (1), remaining accuracy limit (1): 5 points.

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record whether you confused a stored record with a usable or correct record. Tomorrow, reconstruct the chain and redo the 2,000-form audit. In one week, repeat the integration analysis with currency instead of quantities. Distinguish a corrected answer from the reason your first attempt failed.
