Competitive strategy: resources, positioning and digital claims

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

Strategy and business models

From means to advantage, not a guaranteed outcome

Strategy concerns broad, future-oriented choices about fields of activity, resources and competition. It relates external opportunities and threats to internal strengths and weaknesses. A daily software setting is not automatically a corporate strategy simply because it uses technology. Corporate choices concern the overall portfolio and direction; business-unit choices concern how a business competes; functional choices support these through skills, financing, technology and operations.

Two lenses help explain competitive advantage. The resource-based view asks which difficult-to-trade, substitute or imitate resources and competences enable superior customer benefit. The market-based view asks how positioning fits the external economic environment. Owning a resource does not establish a customer benefit; finding an attractive position does not establish the ability to serve it.

The lecture’s means–ends graphic puts both resource base and market positioning before competitive advantage and sustainable success. Read the arrows as a proposed explanatory relationship, not a proof that a purchased asset guarantees profit.

The two inputs matter together: a distinctive maintenance capability helps only if customers value its outcome and the firm can capture enough of that value. Conversely, a low-price position is difficult to sustain when its required cost structure is missing.

Digital advantage and a source tension

The digital-age argument emphasizes complementary connected products, platforms, network effects and switching costs. A network effect means that participation can change the benefit of an offering for other participants; simply adding software does not demonstrate such an effect. Switching costs concern the burden of changing provider, not an automatic increase in customer welfare.

Source tension: the strong claim that advantage no longer comes from cost or differentiation sits alongside the preceding market-/resource-based discussion. Treat it as a strategic argument about expanded competitive scope, not a universal finding that costs and differentiated benefits have ceased to matter. The deck also presents a critical historical position about business-model language. These viewpoints require comparison, not silent reconciliation into a guaranteed platform recipe.

Own worked comparison: assume equal annual volume of 1,000 maintenance subscriptions. A specialist charges €120 each, incurs €60 variable cost per customer and €40,000 fixed cost: €120,000 − €60,000 − €40,000 = €20,000 annual operating result. A low-price design charges €90, incurs €45 variable cost and €35,000 fixed cost: €90,000 − €45,000 − €35,000 = €10,000. The specialist wins this assumed comparison, but demand at each price, quality and imitation are not established. One year’s positive result does not prove sustainable advantage.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment tasks. Write your attempt before opening a hint or solution. Assess reasoning, units, interpretation and assumptions with the criteria; keyword matching does not prove understanding.

1. Reconstruct means and ends

Your attempt: Explain one resource-based and one market-based question for a maintenance service. Why is an exclusive dataset not enough to demonstrate sustainable success?

Optional hint

Connect a resource to a customer benefit and an actual competitive context.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Resource question: can a difficult-to-imitate diagnostic competence improve reliability? Market question: is there a customer segment valuing that reliability at a viable price relative to alternatives? Exclusivity alone does not establish useful data, delivery capability, demand or value capture. Competitors and conditions can change.

Assessment: Resource lens, positioning lens, causal gap, sustainability condition: 1 point each (4).

2. Compare at lower volume

Your attempt: Using the worked designs, compute annual operating results at 600 subscriptions each. Calculate the specialist’s equality volume with the low-price design if their volumes are identical.

Optional hint

The contribution margins are €60 and €45 per subscription; fixed costs remain unchanged.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Specialist: 600 × €60 − €40,000 = −€4,000/year. Low-price: 600 × €45 − €35,000 = −€8,000/year. Equal results solve 60q − 40,000 = 45q − 35,000, so q = 333.33 subscriptions/year; for whole identical volumes, the specialist is better from 334. Better relative performance can still mean an absolute loss.

Assessment: −€4,000, −€8,000, equality equation, 333.33/334 with unit, relative versus absolute: 1 point each (5).

3. Repair a digital inevitability claim

Your attempt: “Our platform has software, so it has network effects; therefore cost no longer matters and our advantage must last.” Diagnose the chain.

Optional hint

A feature, a participation mechanism and a durable economic result are different claims.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Software alone does not show that additional participation benefits others. Demonstrate the mechanism and relevant participant groups. Even a network effect does not remove operating costs or prove value capture. Sustainability requires evidence about alternatives, imitation and changing conditions. The lecture’s broad digital claim should remain a qualified viewpoint, not a universal guarantee.

Assessment: No feature/effect equivalence, participation mechanism, cost/value-capture gap, sustainability/source limit: 1 point each (4).

4. Transfer under partner dependence

Your attempt: The specialist now requires a partner service costing €25 per customer, without changing price or fixed costs. Compare both designs at 1,000 subscriptions and calculate the specialist’s own break-even volume.

Optional hint

Only the specialist’s margin changes, from €60 to €35.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Specialist: 1,000 × €35 − €40,000 = −€5,000/year. Low-price remains +€10,000/year. Specialist break-even is 40,000/35 = 1,142.857 subscriptions/year, requiring at least 1,143 whole subscriptions for a positive result. An attractive resource story does not offset the assumed economic disadvantage; demand, quality and partner continuity still need investigation.

Assessment: €35 margin, −€5,000, +€10,000 comparison, 1,143/equation, conditional strategic interpretation: 1 point each (5).

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record one error in your first attempt, its cause and the corrected explanation. Correct final numbers without a justified calculation earn no reasoning credit. Tomorrow: reconstruct the mechanism and task 2 unaided. In one week: repeat the partner-cost comparison at €15 rather than €25 per specialist customer. Explain the changed assumption before calculating.
