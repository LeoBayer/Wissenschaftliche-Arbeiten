Transistors: density is not device performance

IT-driven Innovation · Course 96437 · WiSe 2026/27

MechanismWorked exampleUnderstand and applySelf-checkSources

Back to IT-driven Innovation

Explain the mechanism

A transistor is essentially a semiconductor-based switch. Bringing large numbers of such switches onto chips supports complex computation in a small physical space. The lecture contrasts this with vacuum-tube electronics and identifies five possibilities: miniaturization, energy-efficient computation, reliability/durability, switching speed and mass production.

These possibilities have different mechanisms. Smaller components support portable devices; less energy per suitable operation can support longer battery life; solid-state construction avoids fragile filaments; rapid switching supports faster computation; manufacturing at scale can lower per-unit production costs. A smaller component does not by itself specify a complete device’s battery life, robustness or price. Displays, radio use, cooling, software and the workload can remain limiting factors.

Transistor count and transistor density must also be kept separate. Density = count / chip area. A larger count on a smaller area implies higher density, but count does not measure useful operations per second. Architecture, workload and the use of the transistors matter. Worked example

From the lecture’s historical values, A10 density is 3,300/125.0 = 26.40 million transistors/mm². A11 density is 4,300/87.7 ≈ 49.03 million/mm²; A17 Pro density is 19,000/103.8 ≈ 183.04 million/mm². The A11/A10 count ratio is about 1.30, while the density ratio is about 1.86 because chip area also falls.

This does not show that an A11 completes every workload 1.86 times as fast. The ratios concern different physical quantities, not measured runtime. The unit conversion matters: a billion transistors is 1,000 million, so dividing 3.3 by 125 without conversion produces billion/mm², not million/mm².

Own simplified energy case: a device has a usable 12 Wh battery and constant average power of 3 W, giving 12 Wh / 3 W = 4 h. If computation power falls from 1 W to 0.5 W while other components stay at 2 W, total power falls to 2.5 W and runtime rises to 4.8 h, not 8 h. This assumes the same workload, usable energy and operating conditions; it is not a measurement of any chip in the table.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment or examination questions. Write your attempt before opening a hint. Use the complete solution and concrete criteria to assess your reasoning; no keyword matching is used. A correct number without its requested reasoning does not earn reasoning credit.

1. Connect hardware to possibilities

Your attempt: From memory, explain the five lecture possibilities and give one reason why a transistor-level improvement need not translate proportionally into a whole-device improvement.

Optional hint

Use component → device → task, not a single universal speed multiplier.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Miniaturization supports portability; energy efficiency can extend battery use; solid-state reliability reduces filament-related failures; faster switching supports computation; scale production supports affordability. A device still includes other components and workload/architecture constraints, so one improved component does not fix all bottlenecks.

Assessment: Miniaturization/energy (1), reliability/speed (1), production (1), whole-device limit (1): 4 points.

2. Compute count, density and runtime separately

Your attempt: Own hypothetical chips: X has 6 billion transistors over 120 mm²; Y has 9 billion over 100 mm². Compute both densities and Y/X density ratio. A separate device uses a 15 Wh battery at constant 3 W: compute runtime. State what the chip data cannot establish.

Optional hint

Convert counts into millions first; Wh divided by W yields hours.

Complete worked answer and criteria

X: 6,000/120 = 50 million/mm². Y: 9,000/100 = 90 million/mm². Density ratio: 90/50 = 1.8. Runtime: 15/3 = 5 h. These data do not establish relative benchmark speed or the power of the chips; runtime uses the independently supplied whole-device power.

Assessment: 50 with units, 90 with units, ratio 1.8, 5 h, inference limit: 1 point each (5).

3. Diagnose two performance shortcuts

Your attempt: A report says: “Count rose 30%, so every application is 30% faster. Halving computation power doubles battery life.” Identify the missing assumptions in both claims.

Optional hint

Useful performance depends on workload; total power includes more than computation.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Count is not a benchmark: architecture and workload must be measured, and not all transistors perform the same role. Halving computation power halves total device power only if all power were computation and other conditions stayed fixed. With substantial display/radio/other power, the runtime gain is smaller.

Assessment: Count/speed distinction (1), workload/architecture (1), component/total power (1), conditional runtime interpretation (1): 4 points.

4. Transfer when another subsystem dominates

Your attempt: Own device: usable battery 20 Wh; computation 1 W; radio/display 4 W. A redesign halves computation power but leaves everything else fixed. Calculate old/new runtime and the percentage gain. Explain a changed usage pattern that could invalidate the estimate.

Optional hint

New total power is 4.5 W, not 2.5 W; compare runtime to its old value.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Old total: 5 W; runtime 4 h. New total: 4.5 W; runtime 20/4.5 ≈ 4.44 h. Gain: (4.44…/4 − 1) × 100 ≈ 11.11%. A heavier workload, more radio use or changed display brightness would break the constant-other-power/same-use assumptions. The calculation isolates one component change, not a universal product forecast.

Assessment: Old total/runtime (1), new total (1), 4.44 h (1), 11.11% (1), changed usage/assumption (1): 5 points.

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record whether you mixed count, density, speed or total power. Tomorrow, recompute the hypothetical densities and battery case without looking. In one week, redo the transfer when radio/display power is only 1 W. Distinguish a corrected answer from the reason your first attempt failed.
