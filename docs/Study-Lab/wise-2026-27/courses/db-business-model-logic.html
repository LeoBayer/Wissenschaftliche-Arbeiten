Business models: value creation, proposition and revenue

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

Strategy and business models

Three connected questions

A business model represents how involved parties and their roles contribute to value generation, what benefit is offered to customers or others, and where revenue comes from. These are connected questions, not three interchangeable slogans.

Architecture of value generation: who performs which activities and supplies which inputs? Value proposition: whose problem is addressed and what benefit is offered? Revenue model: who pays, for what, and through which revenue logic? A user can receive a benefit without being the payer. Conversely, a revenue stream alone does not explain why anyone would pay or how delivery is organized.

The model makes interdependencies visible so people can generate and evaluate ideas and communicate them to partners, employees, customers and investors. It supports strategic planning but does not by itself settle competitive positioning or show a sustainable advantage. Strategy asks about consequential choices in a competitive environment; a business model represents the value and revenue logic used to make such choices discussable.

Work through a repair-service model

Own hypothetical example: a service connects households needing reliable repairs with independent technicians. The benefit is access to coordinated repairs, not merely possession of an app. Technicians deliver repairs; the platform organizes matching and support; households book and pay. The platform retains a €12 fee per completed booking. Technician remuneration is outside the platform’s revenue in this simplified net-fee model, not counted twice as platform income.

At 500 completed bookings/month, fee revenue is 500 × €12 = €6,000. Support and payment processing cost €4 per booking, and fixed operating costs are €3,000/month. Contribution is €8 per booking; operating result is 500 × €8 − €3,000 = €1,000/month. Break-even is 375 completed bookings/month. These are own teaching assumptions, not an official table or evidence of real demand. Taxes, setup costs and working capital are not modeled.

Now connect the components: replacing technicians with an automated scheduling feature does not itself deliver the physical repair. Raising the retained fee can change technician willingness to participate and the household’s price. The architecture and value proposition therefore constrain a revenue-model change. A neatly completed model is a set of linked hypotheses to test, not proof of viability.

Read historical criticism as a warning

The deck juxtaposes a critical position about vague business-model language with an explicit operational definition. Preserve that tension. The useful response is to state actors, benefits and revenue mechanisms precisely and examine their interdependencies, not to conclude either that every model is useless or that a model automatically replaces strategy. This page covers the definition and relationship only; it does not claim to cover the later Canvas, STOF or St. Gallen methods.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment tasks. Write your attempt before opening a hint or solution. Assess reasoning, units, interpretation and assumptions with the criteria; keyword matching does not prove understanding.

1. Rebuild the logic without a template

Your attempt: For the repair case name a benefit, two actor roles and the revenue source. Explain why “we have an app” is incomplete.

Optional hint

A technology is not the same as the customer’s problem or the payer’s reason to pay.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Households obtain coordinated access to repairs. Technicians perform repairs while the platform matches bookings and provides support. The platform earns a retained fee per completed booking. An app describes a possible interface, not the benefit, delivery responsibilities or revenue logic.

Assessment: Benefit, two roles, revenue source, technology/logic distinction: 1 point each (4).

2. Compute and interpret an operating result

Your attempt: At 300 completed bookings under the worked assumptions, calculate revenue, contribution and operating result. Find the completed volume needed to earn €2,000/month operating result.

Optional hint

Use €12 revenue but only €8 contribution per booking.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Revenue is 300 × €12 = €3,600/month. Contribution is 300 × €8 = €2,400/month. Operating result is €2,400 − €3,000 = −€600/month. For €2,000 result: 8q − 3,000 = 2,000, giving 625 completed bookings/month. This is a required volume, not evidence that demand exists.

Assessment: €3,600, €2,400, −€600, 625/equation, demand/unit interpretation: 1 point each (5).

3. Diagnose two category errors

Your attempt: “Our value proposition is the €12 commission. Five hundred bookings prove a sustainable competitive strategy.” Correct the two statements and propose a missing test.

Optional hint

Separate a revenue source from a benefit, and current volume from durable competitive advantage.

Complete worked answer and criteria

The retained fee describes revenue; the proposed benefit is coordinated repair access, whose quality customers must actually value. An assumed or observed volume alone does not establish durable advantage over alternatives. Test customer benefit, partner delivery and willingness to pay against relevant alternatives; one positive operating result cannot establish sustainability.

Assessment: Fee/revenue distinction, customer benefit, volume/sustainability gap, concrete comparative test: 1 point each (4).

4. Transfer from bookings to completions

Your attempt: Suppose there are 500 bookings but 20% cancel. Only completed bookings yield the €12 fee and incur the €4 variable cost; canceled bookings have no additional cost. Calculate the result and the minimum whole bookings for strictly positive operating result at the same completion rate. Explain what changes if cancellations require support.

Optional hint

Expected completions are 0.8 times bookings. Do not charge the fee on cancellations.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Expected completions: 400/month. Operating result: 400 × €8 − €3,000 = €200/month. Expected result as a function of bookings b is 6.4b − 3,000; strictly positive requires b > 468.75, hence at least 469 whole bookings in this expectation model. This is not a guarantee of realized completions. If cancellations require support, include those incremental costs and recalculate; the €200 result is otherwise overstated.

Assessment: 400, €200, inequality/469, expected-versus-realized limit, cancellation-cost correction: 1 point each (5).

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record one error in your first attempt, its cause and the corrected explanation. Correct final numbers without a justified calculation earn no reasoning credit. Tomorrow: reconstruct the mechanism and task 2 unaided. In one week: repeat the cancellation case with a 70% completion rate and distinguish expected bookings from guaranteed completions. Explain the changed assumption before calculating.
