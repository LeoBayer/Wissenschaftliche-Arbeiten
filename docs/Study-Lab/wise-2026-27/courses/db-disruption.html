Disruptive innovation: a process, not a buzzword

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

Start with access, not spectacular technologyUnderstand and applySelf-check (SC — select one answer)Source and limits

Digital Business overview

Learning goal

Explain disruption through entry position and development over time, and distinguish it from generic technological success.

Start with access, not spectacular technology

Disruptive innovations can make a product or service accessible to people previously excluded by money or skill. Early disruptive businesses may have lower gross margins, smaller target markets and simpler offerings that look weak on established performance measures. These characteristics are possible early-stage features, not a checklist that mechanically proves disruption.

More specifically, disruptive innovation starts in low-end or new-market footholds; mainstream adoption follows when quality meets mainstream standards. The process matters. A successful digital service is not disruptive solely because it grows quickly.

Own teaching example: a simple bookkeeping tool initially serves small clubs unable to afford specialist software. It later improves sufficiently to attract larger organizations. This is a candidate disruption story, not a proven case: one would need evidence of its initial customers, their alternatives, quality development and later mainstream adoption. A premium tool launched directly at the best-served corporate customers lacks the proposed foothold evidence.

Why can incumbents be vulnerable despite advantages?

Experience-curve advantages arise through cumulative output and learning, including standardization, labour efficiency and better equipment use. Cumulative output is not simply calendar age. An entrant may struggle to beat an incumbent on exactly the incumbent’s established dimensions.

Yet established firms can improve offerings beyond some customers’ needs, producing solutions that are too expensive or inconvenient for them. Sustaining existing performance trajectories can leave room for simpler alternatives. This is a mechanism, not a prediction that established firms must fail.

The historical IBM teaching case connects a mainframe-to-PC shift with different margins, prices, customer numbers, marketing and employee mindsets. It illustrates why organizational adaptation can matter alongside engineering. Its figures are historical lecture-case values, not current market data.

A boundary case: Uber

Under Christensen’s foothold and quality-development criteria, the answer to whether Uber qualifies as disruptive is no. The source first poses a question and then supplies that theory-specific answer, rather than making contradictory claims. The generic meaning “industry-changing” is broader than this theory’s definition.

Creative destruction is a broader lens

Schumpeter’s creative destruction describes a broader process: economic structures change as new activities replace old ones. Do not treat this broad account as an interchangeable diagnostic test for Christensen-style disruption. In an answer, name the framework before classifying a case.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment tasks. Write your attempt before opening a hint or answer. Assess reasoning, units, interpretation and assumptions using the criteria; keyword matching does not demonstrate understanding.

1. Rebuild the evolutionary argument

Your attempt: Explain how an entrant can initially look unattractive on incumbent metrics yet become a disruption candidate. Distinguish a new-market foothold from merely attracting fewer customers.

Optional hint

Start with who previously could not use the established offering, then trace quality and adoption.

Complete worked answer and criteria

A simpler affordable offering may enable previously excluded users to participate, while low margins and small initial markets make it unattractive to incumbents. Improvement may later meet mainstream requirements and support mainstream adoption. A small customer count alone proves neither nonconsumption nor a low-end foothold: identify prior alternatives, unmet/overserved needs and the later trajectory. An incumbent can still respond; failure is not inevitable.

Assessment: Access/foothold, initial unattractiveness, quality/adoption sequence, evidence/contingency boundary: 1 point each (4).

2. Test a candidate with comparable metrics

Your attempt: Own bookkeeping pilot: before entry, 150 of 200 clubs use no specialist software; 50 use incumbent software. A simple entrant serves 120 of the former nonusers. Two years later it meets a stated accuracy standard and wins 40 of 100 sampled mainstream organizations. Calculate relevant adoption shares and explain what they do and do not show.

Optional hint

Each share needs its own population; meeting one standard is not the whole causal history.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Initial adoption among previous nonusers: 120/150 = 80%; among all sampled clubs: 120/200 = 60%. Later mainstream adoption: 40/100 = 40%. These observations support access for nonusers and later mainstream adoption, consistent with a new-market pathway. They do not establish why users adopted, all relevant quality requirements, representative market shares or incumbent displacement. Examine prior barriers, comparable longitudinal quality and customer histories before treating this as a proven disruption case.

Assessment: 80% denominator, 60% denominator, 40% denominator, mechanism link, missing evidence: 1 point each (5).

3. Repair two category errors

Your attempt: “Our premium platform grew rapidly, therefore it is disruptive; the incumbent is thirty years old, therefore it has thirty times the experience.” Diagnose both claims and specify better evidence.

Optional hint

Separate market success from foothold, and calendar time from cumulative output.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Rapid premium growth does not show low-end/new-market entry or later quality catch-up. Trace original customers and alternatives, then quality and mainstream adoption over time. Calendar age is not cumulative output: compare relevant cumulative production and learning processes, not years alone. Even output-based experience does not guarantee superiority in a different production system or customer segment.

Assessment: Success/foothold separation, trajectory evidence, age/output separation, learning/context evidence: 1 point each (4).

4. Transfer when the entry position changes

Your attempt: Replace the pilot history: the entrant initially serves 120 premium corporate customers already well served by incumbents; it later offers a cheap club version. Growth and quality are otherwise identical. Does the earlier disruption conclusion survive? Contrast creative destruction and propose the next investigation.

Optional hint

Later low prices do not rewrite the initial foothold.

Complete worked answer and criteria

No: the supplied history no longer supports the original new-market entry argument. Premium entry among already well-served customers fails to demonstrate either specified foothold; identical growth is insufficient. The firm might participate in broader creative destruction if new activities replace old structures, but that requires evidence and is not the same diagnostic. Investigate whether the later club business is a distinct trajectory with genuine prior nonconsumption, relevant quality development and eventual mainstream adoption. Do not declare it disruptive solely because it is cheaper.

Assessment: Changed initial foothold, no inference from growth, distinct broader framework, separate trajectory investigation, evidence/price limit: 1 point each (5).

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record one incorrect denominator, missing cost or unsupported causal link from your first attempt and correct its explanation. A correct label or final number without reasoning earns no reasoning credit. Tomorrow: reconstruct tasks 1 and 2 unaided. In one week: repeat the transfer with 60 rather than 40 mainstream adopters; explain why a larger share still cannot repair missing foothold evidence.
