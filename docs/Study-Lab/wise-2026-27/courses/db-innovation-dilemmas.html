Four innovation dilemmas

Digital Business · Course 95696 · WiSe 2026/27

Where does the impulse come from?Understand and applySelf-check (MC — select all correct answers)Source and limits

Digital Business overview

Learning goal

Use four separate dimensions to describe an innovation choice rather than assigning one all-purpose label.

Where does the impulse come from?

Technology push begins with new technological or scientific knowledge. Market pull emphasizes users and actual use, including lead users and frugal innovation for ordinary users. Push and pull are best understood as extremes to balance actively, not mutually exclusive company identities.

Own teaching example: a new low-power sensor enables a service (push), while tenants’ demand for fewer unnoticed leaks guides its design (pull). A technical opportunity and a user need can shape the same project.

What changes: product or process?

Product innovation changes features of the final product or service; process innovation changes how it is produced or distributed. A qualitative model illustrates a shift in their relative importance as an industry develops:

The vertical axis is innovation rate, the horizontal axis time. Product innovation peaks earlier in this qualitative model; process innovation becomes more important later. The marked dominant design helps explain the transition. The graph provides no numerical forecasts or fixed transition date. On a small screen, open the full-size figure to inspect its labels.

The model suggests conditional strategic implications: emerging industries typically favour product innovation, maturing ones process innovation. Entrants may have opportunities before a dominant design stabilizes or when it collapses; incumbents may benefit during stability. These are tendencies, not universal laws.

Own example continued: adding leak-location accuracy changes service features (product); automating sensor installation scheduling changes delivery (process). One project can include both.

Who contributes knowledge?

Open innovation deliberately imports and exports knowledge to improve innovation; crowdsourcing is one example. “Open” does not simply mean a product is free. The open/closed balance depends on competitive rivalry, one-shot innovation and tightly linked innovation. These factors do not supply a universal scoring rule on their own.

Technology or business model?

Business model innovation includes changing how an offering is sold, not only changing the product or service itself. Opportunities can be explored using value chains, value networks and the business model canvas as diagnostic tools. Own example: improving sensor accuracy is technological; changing from selling devices to a maintenance subscription changes the business model. A subscription is not automatically better: explain value creation and payment rather than merely naming the label.

Understanding pathway

These are own exercises, not official assignment tasks. Write your attempt before opening a hint or answer. Assess reasoning, units, interpretation and assumptions using the criteria; keyword matching does not demonstrate understanding.

1. Separate four dimensions

Your attempt: A sensor firm uses new laboratory knowledge and tenant interviews, adds leak-location accuracy, improves installation scheduling, exchanges interface knowledge with partners and switches to subscriptions. Classify each change on the four dimensions. Explain why one label is insufficient.

Optional hint

An impulse, an offering, a knowledge boundary and a revenue arrangement answer different questions.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Laboratory knowledge is technology push; tenant use needs provide market pull. Better location accuracy changes the product/service; scheduling changes delivery processes. Deliberate partner knowledge exchange is an open-innovation element, even if other knowledge stays closed. A subscription changes the selling/business model; improved sensing is technological. These descriptions coexist: none logically determines the others.

Assessment: Push/pull distinction, product/process distinction, knowledge-flow explanation, technology/selling distinction: 1 point each (4).

2. Calculate a selling-model comparison

Your attempt: Own simplified one-year case: selling 100 sensors yields €200 each, with €120 delivery cost each. A subscription for the same 100 installations yields €25 per month for 12 months; delivery still costs €120 each, service costs €8 per month each, and setup costs €4,000 once. Calculate both contributions before other overhead and the subscription price for equality. No churn, discounting or capacity differences.

Optional hint

Keep the installed base and time horizon identical; subtract the one-off setup only once.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Sales: 100 × (200 − 120) = €8,000. Subscription revenue: 100 × 12 × 25 = €30,000; costs: €12,000 + €9,600 + €4,000 = €25,600, so contribution is €4,400. Equality with sales requires 1,200p − 25,600 = 8,000: p = €28 per installation-month. At €25, subscription contributes €3,600 less despite higher revenue. This is not total profit or a universal verdict on subscriptions.

Assessment: Sales contribution, subscription revenue/costs, €4,400 and €3,600 gap, equality equation/€28 unit, model boundary: 1 point each (5).

3. Diagnose a strategy shortcut

Your attempt: A manager claims: “Our industry is mature, so we must stop product innovation. Our free app proves our innovation is open.” Correct both claims and identify evidence needed for the knowledge-flow claim.

Optional hint

A qualitative tendency is not a prohibition; price is not knowledge exchange.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Maturity can increase the relative importance of process innovation, not eliminate worthwhile product changes. The qualitative graph supplies neither a transition date nor a numerical budget rule. Free access to an app does not establish deliberate import/export of knowledge. Document actual partner contributions, outward knowledge sharing and their role in improving innovation; the price alone is insufficient.

Assessment: Relative versus exclusive claim, graph limit, price/knowledge distinction, suitable evidence: 1 point each (4).

4. Transfer to a shorter customer relationship

Your attempt: Keep the subscription price at €25 and delivery/setup costs unchanged, but every installation pays and receives service for only six months, then leaves. There is no replacement or salvage. Recalculate and propose a justified response without calling every subscription a failure.

Optional hint

Revenue and recurring service costs shorten together; delivery and setup do not.

Complete worked answer and criteria

Revenue: 100 × 6 × 25 = €15,000. Costs: €12,000 + 100 × 6 × 8 + €4,000 = €20,800. Contribution: −€5,800, versus €8,000 for the unchanged sales alternative. For equality on this six-month horizon: 600p − 20,800 = 8,000, hence p = €48/month. Test willingness to pay or a separately justified setup fee/longer retention, including adoption effects; do not simply assume customers accept €48. This is a changed own scenario, not a prediction from the industry graph.

Assessment: Revenue/costs, −€5,800, comparison, €48 equality, conditional response: 1 point each (5).

Error reflection and delayed retrieval

Maximum 18 self-assessed points (4 + 5 + 4 + 5). Record one incorrect denominator, missing cost or unsupported causal link from your first attempt and correct its explanation. A correct label or final number without reasoning earns no reasoning credit. Tomorrow: reconstruct tasks 1 and 2 unaided. In one week: repeat the transfer with nine paid months instead of six; rederive the equal-contribution monthly price.
